Visual ethos · Style guide

Clear systems, visible rules.

The visual side of how I present myself: a game designer and public health student who builds systems people can read at a glance.

Ethos

Game design and public health share a question: when a system is built well, how does it change what people do? My visual language answers it the same way my work does. Rules are visible, structure is shown instead of hidden, and nothing is decorative unless it also explains.

The look borrows from technical drawing, not from either field’s usual costumes: a visible grid, one type family, measurement ticks, and two signal colours that mark which part of my work you are in.

Show the structure

Grid paper, rules and numbered steps make the system behind each page visible.

One idea per signal

Vermilion marks game work and teal marks health work. Everything else stays ink and paper.

Explain, don’t decorate

Every line, tick and colour has a job. If it does not help someone read the page, it is removed.

Avoiding the stereotypes

What I am avoiding

  • Pixel fonts, neon glows, controller icons and “press start” copy for games.
  • Stethoscopes, crosses, DNA helices and blue gradients for health.
  • Stock photos of people pointing at laptops.

What I use instead

  • Plain structure: grids, rules, numbered steps and labelled diagrams.
  • My own artifacts as the imagery: diagrams, screenshots and playtest evidence.
  • Colour as a signal that tells you where you are, not as decoration.

Grid and spacing

Pages sit on a 12-column grid, up to 72rem wide. Spacing comes from an 8px unit, so every gap is a multiple of it. Rows of cards use an auto-fit grid, so they wrap from four across to one without media queries.

Bio block

I’m Kizito Anozie, a fifth-year Health Sciences student at SFU in the Public Health and Data pathway, and a game designer in training. The two fields ask the same question: how does a well-built system change what people do? In games, I design levels and mechanics that teach through feedback. In health, I work with data to understand patterns. I build clean, responsive interfaces to show that thinking, and I’m exploring veterinary medicine because I love animals.

Accessibility and behaviour

  • Text meets WCAG AA contrast. Signal colours are never the only way information is shown: tags and labels always carry words.
  • Every page has a skip link, one h1, ordered headings, landmarks and a visible focus ring.
  • Layouts wrap on their own and are tested from 360px to desktop widths with no sideways scroll.
  • Motion is limited to short colour transitions, and smooth scrolling turns off for visitors who prefer reduced motion.
